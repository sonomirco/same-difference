Long runs aren't new in this industry. We've always left machines working overnight: energy models, structural analysis, clash detection, render farms. What will change is the kind of work we're handing them. A risk question is never in one document; it lives across a drawing, a clause, a model element, a submittal, and a code, and none of those reference each other. Answering it means normalising, reconciling, and translating between representations that never take the same shape twice. And that fragmentation isn't neglect: it buys the division of liability, so every handoff loses context because controlled information flow across responsibility boundaries is the point of the arrangement. What has to change isn't the fragmentation but our knowledge of it: leave every source where it sits, and make it known that it exists and what it holds.

A contractor prices a design-build job on a tender window measured in weeks, before anyone has read the full set, and covers something like 10% of the risk in review. The shortfall doesn't disappear; it surfaces at detail design, when the budget goes. In no version of this do enough people read enough documents in time.

An overnight agent run can hand back the risks and the gaps in the information in time to price the bid. It also leaves behind a map of the document set that stays open to questions: where a clause is answered, which drawing governs, what was never issued at all. These runs are long for a second reason too: they can check themselves. Analyse, review the output against defined criteria, re-run, converge. The duration is the verification, and a fast answer is the unchecked one. Uber Eats shows what that costs. To improve a photograph of a takeaway meal, an image passes a classifier deciding whether to touch it at all, an editor that retries with a new prompt each time, a quality gate scoring faithfulness, naturalness and realism, then a publish check; when the gate keeps failing, the photo isn't published.

**Confidence in a long run doesn't come from a better model; it comes from giving the model less room to decide at every step.**

Many small agents, each doing one specific thing, fenced by guardrails and measured against a deterministic test. Farms of hundreds of them, discovering what the others can do, composed rather than left to compose themselves.

Engineering wrote its criteria down failure by failure over a century: codes, standards, design criteria, acceptance limits. But a clause in a standard isn't a working gate until someone turns it into one, and that conversion is most of the job. What agents can then check is coverage, arithmetic, and anything with a codified criterion behind it: every drawing referenced, every clause read, every quantity reconciled, every clearance and load verified. What the exposure means, and whether to accept it, stays with a person, and not only because the judgment is hard. We pay for the name that certifies the output, not for the tool that produced it. So the product of a long run isn't speed; it's evidence, a trail of what was checked against what, which is what that person needs before they'll sign.
