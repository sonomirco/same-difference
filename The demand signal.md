I notice a similar level of crowding in ConTech: tools for takeoffs, bids, permitting, document comparison, scope review, and compliance.

These are real problems. They consume time, create risk, and decide who wins work. They are also the parts of construction that management has made easiest to represent.

That legibility was manufactured. Todd Zabelle calls the middle of the last century Era 2, the era of predictability, when critical path scheduling, earned value, and phase gates were assembled to make capital projects forecastable. Its lasting effect was to harden the separation between planning and doing. Software has been selling into that layer ever since.

He describes visiting a large project where houses in the surrounding area were being bought and demolished to make laydown space for structural steel and pipe spools, alongside rows of flatbed trailers holding the rest. The construction manager was proud of the materials system that produced this: laydown allocation on a whiteboard, spreadsheet trackers, bills of material pulled automatically from the 3D model, work packages defined 12 weeks ahead, bills of material at six weeks, kitting at three, delivery at one.

Logistics kept asking the site what it would need weeks in advance. The site kept answering that it would take anything; it just needed to burn hours.

Every party was paid correctly throughout. The supplier delivered, the specialty contractor took its markup, the planner booked the spend as progress, and the owner saw progress being made.

The system had information, a plan, and activity that could be reported. What it lacked was any reliable link between what was supplied and what could actually be built.

**The digitised planning layer is not neutral about what happens on site; it is better than paper ever was at the thing that hurts production.**

Faster takeoffs and more documents checked make the managerial layer quicker and more persuasive while labour availability, material flow, trade sequencing, and changing conditions stay where they were.

What would matter is not another licence. I have argued before that adoption improves when people consume an outcome rather than tooling they have to assemble themselves, and that infrastructure enabling a whole class of work beats a feature that speeds up one task. Applied here, that means something that sits inside the production system, carries responsibility for what it produces, and is paid on the result rather than on access.

Almost nobody builds that, and the reason is arithmetic rather than imagination. Software is roughly 2 percent of a construction company's costs; materials and labour are almost all of the rest. Selling a licence means competing for the 2 percent. Selling an outcome means competing for the other 98 and underwriting a result in conditions you do not control.

The question for any AI product is therefore larger than whether it saves time in an existing workflow. Does it change how the project is produced? Does it put better information and decision-making closer to the work? Can feedback from the site alter what is planned, procured, and released?

Back on that project, the fix was not a tool either. Production packages were cut to one shift's work for one crew, bills of material to install went from six weeks to one, and the supply schedule was deleted and replaced with a signal from site.

The demand signal moved to the people holding the tools.
