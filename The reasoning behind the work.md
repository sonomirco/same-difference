I've been listening to a few podcasts that approached AI and software from different angles, but kept converging on the same question: what makes our data and information useful? It still feels unresolved and gets less attention than the current focus on building tools. As writing code becomes more accessible, the harder advantage to replicate may sit in proprietary knowledge, feedback from users, specialist expertise and how well a product serves a real need.

That left me thinking about AEC. We are getting better at creating tools, but how much progress are we making in organising the knowledge those tools need?

Here are a few reflections from those conversations.

We have years of models, drawings, specifications, emails and project records. Yet we still struggle to learn across projects, and teams often recreate work the firm has already done. Some of that information is useful; some is outdated, duplicated or missing the context needed to interpret it.

The biggest gap is that we tend to capture what was delivered, but much less of how and why it was decided: the alternatives considered, assumptions made, constraints encountered and trade-offs accepted. Those are often the most valuable parts of an experienced engineer's contribution, and they rarely appear in the final deliverable.

This is why I find the distinction between three systems useful:

- *System of record* stores what happened: the drawings, models, documents and project history.
- *System of action* uses that information to execute and coordinate work: routing reviews, preparing RFIs or moving information between tools.
- *System of intelligence* connects decisions with outcomes, surfaces patterns and helps people assess risks and make better judgments.

The opportunity is to connect all three. A workflow can also capture why someone accepted a recommendation, changed an assumption or rejected an option. That context can help the next team decide when to reuse a previous solution.

AI can help organise and retrieve our archives, but making information searchable doesn't make it trustworthy. We still need to know where it came from, whether it remains applicable and whether the underlying decision produced a good outcome.

There is an organisational challenge too. Contracts and commercial incentives can discourage the sharing that connected workflows depend on. Capturing reasoning needs an owner and a purpose, alongside the technology.

A practical starting point is one recurring question or delivery problem. Identify the information needed to address it, test whether it improves the decision, and build context capture into the workflow as people work.

**We need to preserve the reasoning behind our work, alongside the work itself.** That could make our project history far more useful for delivery, commercial decisions and developing the next generation of engineers.
