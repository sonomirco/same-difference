Tony Fadell’s point about voice and maps applies to drawings in construction.

The issue is not whether the technology exists. It does. The issue is whether a digital tool can carry the same density of information, with the same speed and reliability, in the conditions where the work actually happens.

On a factory floor or a job site, the drawing still wins because it is immediate. No battery. No signal. No app to load. No need to take your gloves off. If it gets wet or torn, you reprint it.

It also does something the model often still does not: it brings together information that lives across systems that do not naturally sit in one place. The drawing is not surviving because construction is behind on technology. It is surviving because it is still the simplest object that can be read, handled, marked up, and trusted in the field.

That is the bar voice has to clear before it replaces the map. Not smarter. Simpler.

The question is where the working environment is controlled enough, and the digital system complete enough, for people to stop reaching for paper.
