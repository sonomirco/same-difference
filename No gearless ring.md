I wroted about what remains of the engineer once AI runs the checkable half of judgment, and then a boxing anime picked the question up from the other side.

In Megalobox, every fighter wears Gear, a powered exoskeleton that adds speed, power, and reach. Joe enters the championship without any. One opponent fights in an AI-assisted Gear that learns his movement mid-fight; Joe beats it by breaking his own rhythm until there is nothing stable left to learn. In the final, Yuri, the champion carrying the most advanced Gear in the sport, has his removed to meet Joe as an equal. The most augmented man in the ring decides the fight only means something without the augmentation.

The gesture works because boxing is judged the way design is judged: by taste. The crowd can see the difference between the augmented and the unaugmented performance. Aurélie Radom argues that taste cannot be delegated, that someone has to judge what deserves to exist; Megalobox is what her argument looks like when it gets to stage its proof. Gearless is legible. Nobody watching Joe wonders how much of it was the machine.

Engineering can't run that experiment. A beam calculated by hand and a beam calculated by an agent are the same beam; the codes that approve it don't ask how it was made, and neither does the client or the insurer. In a domain where quality was codified precisely so it wouldn't depend on any one person's eye, refusing augmentation proves nothing, because no audience can see the difference.

The obvious objection is that engineering does display unaided skill: chartership exams, technical interviews, the senior engineer sense-checking a result on the back of an envelope. But those are gyms, not rings. They watch rehearsal. Nobody approves a bridge because the engineer performed beautifully in the exam room, and no client pays extra for a structure computed by hand. Joe's gesture depends on going gearless where the title is decided. **Engineering has gearless gyms but no gearless ring: unaided skill is displayed only where nothing is at stake.**

That's why the human-made premium emerging elsewhere will never arrive for us. Chess protects over-the-board play with arbiters and metal detectors; galleries certify the hand of the artist; wherever provenance is visible and the audience cares, "human-made" is becoming a label worth paying for. Engineering's artefact carries no provenance a buyer can see, so its human element can only be signed, never performed.

And yet a gearless move still exists in engineering. It just isn't public. It happens in the minute before the verdict, when you form your own view of the answer before looking too long at the machine's. Addy Osmani calls losing that moment cognitive surrender: the AI's output becomes your output, and there is nothing left you feel you need to check. Joe fought his fight in front of a full stadium. Ours happens alone, unwatched, every time we decide whether we actually reviewed the thing we are about to approve. Going gearless was never about refusing the tool; it's refusing to fall asleep inside it. The only difference is that nobody will ever see whether we won.
